\begin{abstract}
Morphological reconstruction in paleobiology has historically been a manual process subject to speculation or subjective interpretation. Fossils provide the geometry, but extrapolating musculature and appearance from living relatives is a complex and difficult task. OsteoGen addresses this limitation by providing a framework for computational paleontology based on neural networks, designed to automate and quantify phenotypic similarities between extinct species and a reference dataset. In the first phase, the system recognizes a skeletal structure model and its pose as input, extracting 14 main anatomical keypoints. Once this is done, the target is geometrically compared with metrics extracted from a dataset of approximately 65 living animals. The model, developed in PyTorch, uses a ResNet18 encoder pre-trained on ImageNet coupled with a decoder to generate keypoint heatmaps, employing the AdamW optimizer and a Weighted MSE Loss function. OsteoGen produces geometric similarity rankings and an optional generative visual reconstruction using Stable Diffusion, ControlNet, and IP-Adapter to blend the textures of the most compatible donor animals. The system outputs a geometric comparison report in JSON and HTML formats, explicitly noting that the resulting percentages represent geometric similarities between normalized silhouettes rather than taxonomic or biological probabilities. Further assumptions or considerations will be left to experts in the field.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Computational Paleontology, Virtual Paleontology following the advent of 3D visualization technologies, relies on Extant Phylogenetic Bracketing (EPB) to infer extinct organisms' traits from their living relatives. \textit{OsteoGen} is the automated evolution of this concept: a deep learning-driven system for morphological and soft-tissue reconstruction \cite{Paleogical_3D_reconstruction}.

Rather than merely classifying skeletal remains by superficial similarity, \textit{OsteoGen} isolates the subject via background removal and Canny-edge canonicalization before extracting anatomical keypoints, then normalizes the geometry by translating the origin to the neck base and scaling by torso or skull length, making different animals geometrically comparable.

Historically, virtual reconstruction of extinct fauna required digitizing fossilized bones and manually applying geometric primitives (cylinders, spheres) to estimate muscle volumes. Today, CNN- and ResNet-based architectures instead denoise CT scan data, segment fossils from the surrounding rock matrix, and classify skeletal elements automatically.

Machine learning in vertebrate paleobiology faces a persistent small-sample-size challenge, given the scarcity of complete fossil specimens; recent work nonetheless reports accuracies approaching 99\% via transfer learning on datasets of merely 200 to 500 images per class \cite{AI_Paleontology}. \textit{OsteoGen} manages this by matching the geometric proportions of the target's segments (head, torso, forelimb, hindlimb, tail) against the 65 database animals to find the most suitable texture donors.


\section{Related Works}

The theoretical foundation of this project lies at the intersection of evolutionary paleobiology and conditional generative computer vision, based on an idea by Dr. Matteo Cesarini, a researcher in nuclear physics.

\subsection{Paleobiological and Phylogenetic Foundations}
Recent discoveries have established clear molecular and morphological links between extinct fauna and extant species: protein sequencing from fossilized remains shows a direct phylogenetic similarity between \textit{Tyrannosaurus rex} and modern avians such as the chicken \cite{asara2007protein, organ2008molecular}, while primitive plumage found in both non-avian theropods and basal ornithischians, corroborated by specimens preserved in amber \cite{xing2016feathered, godefroit2014jurassic}, shows soft-tissue traits were broadly distributed across dinosaur lineages. This supports the core hypothesis of this work: if extinct species share ancestry with extant macrocategories such as modern birds, computational analysis of their skeletal structures can reveal latent morphological correlations invisible to the human eye.

\subsection{Conditioned Image Synthesis}
This project builds on established image-to-image translation and conditioned rendering methods: conditional adversarial networks (e.g., Pix2Pix) and the PatchGAN discriminator \cite{isola2017image} preserve high-frequency local detail by evaluating image patches rather than global structure, while ControlNet \cite{zhang2023adding} uses "zero convolutions" to lock a structural input (keypoint maps, skeletal edges) while exploiting a pre-trained diffusion model's generative capabilities, the framework this project relies on to map a structural pose to a photorealistic, anatomically coherent output.


\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
The approach, and the locally-trained models behind the experimental results, changed three times: from a U-Net extracting skeletal features directly, to a prompt-conditioned ControlNet fine-tuned on the 65 selected species, and finally to a keypoint-based method using a ResNet18, each detailed in its own subsection below. Skeleton and living-animal images obtained from the internet were isolated, placed on a black background, and resized to 512$\times$512 as required by the chosen models.

\subsection{Version 0}
The initial experimental phase functioned as an ablation study, evaluating three distinct image-to-image translation architectures to establish a generative baseline.

The first model, a simple U-Net trained from scratch via pixel-wise L1 (Mean Absolute Error) regression, served as proof that minimizing absolute error without a discriminator inherently produces blurry, averaged textures when multiple plausible phenotypic solutions exist.

To counter this, the second model adopted a full Pix2Pix Conditional GAN: a deeper, 6-level U-Net Generator paired with a PatchGAN Discriminator that evaluates local 70$\times$70 pixel patches rather than the whole image, penalizing unrealistic textures; this adversarial signal was combined with a scaled ($\times$100) L1 loss to preserve structural fidelity.

The third model shifted to a Latent Diffusion approach with ControlNet and Stable Diffusion 1.5: to prevent catastrophic forgetting, the pre-trained Foundation Model (VAE, U-Net, text encoder) was entirely frozen and only the ControlNet weights were updated (AdamW), trained unconditionally on an empty prompt so the cross-attention layers process a semantically empty tensor, forcing the network to generate the phenotype from the injected skeletal geometry alone, rather than semantic text hints.

Early stopping was used for all three training runs to prevent overfitting. Since ControlNet's results were initially most promising, we re-tested it on a lion (a similar feline is in the dataset) with both unconditional and generic-prompt generation; results remained unsatisfactory.

\subsection{Version 1}
After analyzing the outputs and comparing several AI approaches, we refined ControlNet by radically changing how the dataset fed textual information to the network.

The \texttt{OsteoDataset} class was upgraded to dynamically generate highly descriptive semantic prompts for each skeleton. Using a static offline translation map to convert Italian filenames to English, the dataset constructed the following string: \textit{"A full-body three-quarter view photo of a \texttt{<animal\_name>}, featuring highly detailed, photorealistic textures, 8K resolution, studio lighting, and fully isolated against a pure black background."}

To prevent the model from ignoring the skeletal geometry in favor of these strong textual cues, we added a stochastic "prompt dropout": a 10\% chance per training step of replacing the prompt with an empty string, exposing the network to both conditioned and unconditioned states, enabling Classifier-Free Guidance at inference and preserving its ability to interpret raw bone structure.

Although this led to modest improvements, results remained unpresentable: testing the T-Rex skeleton with contrasting prompts (\textit{``unknown biological animal''} vs. an explicit \textit{``Tyrannosaurus Rex dinosaur... reptilian skin''}) produced highly erratic outputs, alternating between sensible images and biologically illogical artifacts, confirming that end-to-end generative AI could not reliably deduce extinct phenotypes without strict geometric constraints.

\subsection{Version 2}
Version 2 reverses the analytical approach entirely, abandoning direct image-to-image translation for a structural and geometric pipeline: instead of a network blindly generating a complete image from a skeleton, it isolates the anatomical data by extracting 14 main keypoints (snout, neck base, shoulders, joints, etc.).

The image preprocessing first isolates the subject using background removal tools and canonicalizes it via Canny edges, applying a letterboxing technique to fit a 512$\times$512 canvas without deforming the original skeletal proportions. The core architecture is a \textit{ResNet18KeypointDetector}, which utilizes a ResNet18 encoder pre-trained on ImageNet coupled with a decoder consisting of five ConvTranspose2d layers. The decoder outputs raw, unactivated logits (no final Sigmoid or ReLU) so that the Weighted MSE loss described below is computed directly on them, producing 14 distinct spatial heatmaps. Training is optimized using AdamW and a Weighted MSE Loss, which assigns a significantly higher penalty (a weight of 50) to the pixels belonging to the keypoint Gaussian distributions, preventing the network from defaulting to background prediction.

During inference, the extracted keypoints undergo a geometric normalization process: the origin is translated to the base of the neck, and the scale is determined by the torso length or, if unavailable, the skull length. This normalization allows the system to accurately compare the geometric proportions of the target's five main segments (head, torso, forelimb, hindlimb, and tail) against the 65 animals in the database using Root Mean Square (RMS) distances. These distances are subsequently converted into geometric similarity percentages using a softmax function, establishing a ranking of the most structurally compatible species.

Finally, an optional generative rendering pipeline handles the visual reconstruction: a structural control map of specific lines and points guides ControlNet, ensuring strict adherence to the input pose, while Stable Diffusion 1.5's IP-Adapter ingests the highest-ranking donor animals' reference images from the geometric retrieval phase, blending their textures, colors, and visual styles into a coherent, geometrically constrained final output.

\subsection{Generalization beyond the training distribution}
Trained on only 65 (later 68, see Section~\ref{sec:ai-use}) skeleton images, the keypoint detector generalizes poorly to unfamiliar body plans. On three added extinct-species examples (\textit{T.\ rex}, \textit{Brachiosaurus}, \textit{Triceratops}), average confidence rose from 0.36,0.51 to 0.83,0.89 after a short fine-tuning run: collapsed keypoints resolved into 13,14 distinct locations out of 14, and retrieval moved from a near-uniform ranking ($\approx$1.5\% per candidate) to a differentiated one, without degrading accuracy on the original species.

This gain did not generalize beyond the added examples' pose and style: a held-out \textit{Allosaurus} photograph (different pose, cluttered background) reproduced the original failure (confidence 0.38), matching the small-sample-size challenge cited in Section~\ref{sec:intro} \cite{AI_Paleontology}, three examples help similar inputs, not broad generalization.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\textit{OsteoGen} is deliberately limited to a compact, manually annotated dataset carefully selected based on bone morphology, consisting of 65 species, which later increased to 68, to test the limits of purely geometric recovery without a large training set. The results are encouraging: differentiated rankings and a measurable improvement in generalization (Section~\ref{sec:experimental-results}) achieved by adding just a few examples of extinct species. The paleobiological hypotheses remain a simplification: we are computer science students, not experts in the field. We view \textit{OsteoGen} as a proof of concept to uncover the latent morphological affinities of dinosaurs with their living or recently extinct relatives.

\section{The Use of AI}
\label{sec:ai-use}

\subsection{Research and conceptual work}
Large Language Models were used to streamline literature reviews, identify relevant scientific papers, and explore existing neural network architectures applicable to our objectives, under continuous human oversight. The conceptual evolution of the three pipeline versions (Sections 3.1-3.3), the choice of architectures, losses, and normalization scheme, and the core training/inference code for all three versions (the U-Net, PatchGAN, and ControlNet baselines in Version 0; the prompt-conditioned ControlNet in Version 1; the \texttt{ResNet18KeypointDetector}, the geometric normalization/retrieval algorithm, and the ControlNet+IP-Adapter rendering pipeline in Version 2) were designed and written by the team, predating any AI-assisted coding session on this codebase. AI was used here only as a reference for library documentation, method syntax, and code validation, as originally stated.

\subsection{AI-assisted engineering after the modeling work was complete}
Separately from the modeling work above, an AI coding assistant (Claude, Anthropic) was used, with the team directing and reviewing the process throughout, to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item build an interactive command-line interface around the already-working Version 2 pipeline (a guided prompt for the input image, per-stage progress reporting, automatic installation of missing Python dependencies, and automatic resolution/hosting of the trained keypoint-detector weights via the Hugging Face Hub) so the pipeline could be run end-to-end by someone unfamiliar with the codebase;
    \item translate the pipeline's console/report output to English;
    \item identify and fix a small number of concrete bugs surfaced only when running the pipeline on a second machine: a non-portable absolute file path baked into the retrieval database by \texttt{build\_DB.py}, a crash in the IP-Adapter rendering path when no donor reference image is available, and a Windows-specific OpenMP/console-encoding issue;
    \item diagnose, via real inference runs, why the keypoint detector produced near-uniform, uninformative retrieval results on the T-Rex/Brachiosaurus test images (average per-keypoint confidence of 0.36-0.51, with multiple distinct joints collapsing onto nearly the same pixel);
    \item source (from openly licensed collections, with attribution recorded in \texttt{dataset\_tools/attributions.csv}), visually annotate, and add three supplementary training images (T-Rex, Brachiosaurus, Triceratops) to the keypoint detector's training set, and run a short fine-tuning pass from the existing trained weights (the results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:experimental-results} and reflect real, measured before/after numbers, including the generalization limitation);
    \item fix a second concrete bug found once a co-author ran the pipeline independently: the weights-resolution logic checked only whether a local copy already existed, so a machine that had run the pipeline once kept silently reusing pre-fine-tuning weights even after improved ones were published, now the Hugging Face Hub copy is always checked first;
    \item audit and clean up the whole repository: remove duplicated dataset copies, intermediate working files, and superseded draft documentation (355MB, verified beforehand to be unused by any code or by this report), translate every remaining Italian string/comment/CLI flag in Version 0, 1 and 2 to English, remove machine-specific absolute paths from Version 0/1's scripts, and refine the final README;
    \item as a final pre-submission pass, remove further uncited planning files and stale pipeline-run outputs left over from earlier development, and proofread this report end-to-end against the actual code and figures, fixing a caption/content mismatch in a figure, relabeling remaining Italian text baked into result plots, removing leftover citation-placeholder artifacts.
\end{itemize}
The keypoints for these three supplementary images were estimated visually by the AI assistant from the reference photograph (rather than through the project's own interactive point-and-click annotation tool used for the original 65 species) and checked by rendering them back onto the image; the team reviewed this before it was used for training. This is a methodological difference from the rest of the dataset's provenance and is disclosed here for that reason.
